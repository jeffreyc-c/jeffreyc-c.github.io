In my work, I take up the challenge of theorizing borderlands as unique geopolitical spaces and local populations as central actors. Knowledge about the macro-level causes and effects of border hardening is abundant, but little is understood about its “on-the-ground” politics. Local communities are unique in their opportunity to engage with people, goods, and institutions across the border. These communities also directly experience changes in how borders are governed. 

Given this position, how are local communities navigating the global trend toward hardened borders? I answer this question on three axes: (1) local contestation and accommodation over border hardening, (2) how people in local communities psychologically adapt to changing borders, and (3) how bottom-up demands influence the content of border policy. The multi-method project encompasses global quantitative analyses with an original geospatial measure of local transnational ties across borders, qualitative fieldwork at the United States–Mexico border, and observational survey tests.

    <p>
      States are increasingly hardening borders. Scholars have uncovered various causes and effects of this phenomenon, but local reactions to it are less understood. This study takes up the challenge of theorizing borderlands as unique geopolitical spaces in order to explain why the communities therein sometimes resist hardening. Borderland communities that are highly transnational often have both the inclination and the capacity to mobilize against new restrictions. Yet, when a hardening strategy is designed in ways that meaningfully preserve interaction and exchange across borders, resistance is less likely. This theory is tested and developed via a mixed-methods design leveraging the COVID-19 pandemic during which most states closed borders. Global quantitative analysis finds that an original geospatial measure of transnationality predicts local protest and riot events against closures. Interviews in two U.S. communities near the border with Mexico, which are highly transnational but did not demonstrate against the closure, show that the policy’s accommodation of key flows mitigated discontent. Such selective bordering, narrow in its targets, ultimately reduces conflict. The findings of this study highlight domestic tensions in international border politics, showing that local communities are focal actors that deserve more attention.
